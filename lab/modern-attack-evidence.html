Research and evidence

What an Attack Result Actually Shows

Compare the access, data, computation and validation behind modern cryptanalytic results.

Keep five quantities separate

A useful attack card identifies the target configuration, attacker access, data requirement, computational work and success criterion. A measured throughput is not an observed full-key recovery, and a theoretical keyspace is not necessarily the effective keyspace of a deployment.

GPU search: which keyspace?

The 2025 ToSC paper “GPU Assisted Brute Force Cryptanalysis of GPRS, GSM, RFID, and TETRA” compares optimized implementations and time–memory trade-offs. Its KASUMI-64 setting uses a repeated 64-bit key in a 128-bit-key primitive. That deployment is not equivalent to searching all independent 128-bit KASUMI keys.

The paper’s TETRA target here is TEA3, not TEA1. Its extrapolated SPECK-96 and TEA3 exhaustive-search costs remain enormous. Reporting fast GPU throughput as a practical break of every listed cipher would erase the paper’s distinctions.

Separate measured RTX 4090 throughput from extrapolated total search time, preprocessing, memory, and probability of success. Hardware and cost assumptions are dated measurements, not current cloud-price quotes.

Primary journal paper (2025) · Institutional full text, PDF pp. 1–3 and 12–15. Compare DES’s finite keyspace and SoDark’s related-tweak attacks.

GPRS faults: physical access changes the model

“Practical Differential Fault Attacks on the GPRS Standard Ciphers” studies GEA-1 and GEA-2 under a random fault model. The authors’ abstract reports validation by simulating the attacks on ChipWhisperer Lite, with the experimental attacks completing within sixteen minutes on a common laptop.

This is evidence about the tested implementation and fault model. It is not a demonstration of passive traffic interception or a universal attack on arbitrary commercial phones. The abstract does not establish that the required faults can be induced in every deployed device. Detailed fault-injection and reinitialization requirements require the full paper, which was unavailable during this review.

Compare the need for controlled physical faults with the data/oracle access in SoDark and the authentication/configuration conditions in NFC. The point of the comparison is to ask what an attacker must actually be able to do.

Zhengting Li and coauthors, IEEE Transactions on Dependable and Secure Computing 23(3), pp. 7129–7142 (2026). The authors’ institutional record identifies a peer-reviewed journal article; ePrint 2026/1806 is a minor revision of that published work. This summary uses the verified abstract and publication records.

Conditional-linear DES: discard data to gain bias

Biham and Perle’s 2018 conditional-linear analysis conditions linear approximations on observable information. Keeping a selected subset can increase statistical bias, trading discarded data against a stronger distinguisher.

One reported full-DES operating point uses about 241.9 known plaintexts and analysis work at 85% success. These are separate quantities even when their exponents coincide. The paper includes statistical/reduced-round experiments and selected full-DES checks; §4.7 counts the candidate keys preceding the correct key instead of executing that search; §4.7.1 reports 79 full-DES data/analysis runs using this distinction. It is not a guaranteed, ciphertext-only or laptop-speed recovery claim.

This is conditional-linear analysis. Crypto Lab’s planned differential–linear composition is a different technique. The museum’s contribution is a dated explanation of the result and its evidence conditions.

Biham and Perle, primary ToSC paper (2018), Tables 1–2 and §4.7–4.7.1, PDF pp. 5–6 and 30–34. Journal full text. Return to the DES exhibit.
