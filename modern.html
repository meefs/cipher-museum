Shannon's Framework

Confusion & Diffusion

Confusion

Making the relationship between key and ciphertext as complex as possible. Caesar has a simple key relationship: C = P + 3. One known pair reveals the entire key.

Modern solution: AES S-boxes are highly non-linear. The S-box has a precise public algebraic definition. Its nonlinearity works with the other round operations; an S-box alone does not make encryption secure.

Diffusion

Spreading each plaintext bit's influence across many ciphertext bits. Caesar does not spread changes across letter positions: change one letter, change exactly one ciphertext letter.

Modern solution: AES combines ShiftRows and MixColumns to spread byte dependencies. Across suitable inputs, changing one bit of a full AES input changes about half the output bits on average; this is not an exact per-message promise or a standalone security proof.

The Modern Landscape

What Replaced Classical Cryptography

⚠️

Taxonomy note: Symmetric and public-key encryption schemes both reversibly transform plaintext into ciphertext. RSA encryption and RSA signatures are distinct schemes with different encoding and padding requirements. Diffie–Hellman establishes shared secrets; signatures authenticate messages; hash functions produce digests. These primitives solve different problems and must be combined with appropriate protocols.

🏛️

Looking for the exhibits? Explore these constructions — DES, Diffie-Hellman, RSA, AES, and SHA-256 — and further exhibits in Hall XI · Modern Cryptography. This page provides the wider context: the journey from classical failure to modern practice, and additional constructions such as ChaCha20, ECDH and post-quantum schemes.

🔐 Ciphers — Plaintext → Ciphertext → Plaintext
AES-256 (Symmetric) → exhibit

128-bit blocks, 256-bit key, 14 rounds. 2²⁵⁶ possible keys; no practical key-recovery attack is known against correctly used full-round AES. Protocol, mode and implementation failures remain separate risks.
ChaCha20 (Stream Cipher)

Designed by Daniel Bernstein. Original ChaCha uses a 64-bit nonce and 64-bit block counter. IETF ChaCha20 (RFC 8439) uses a 256-bit key, 96-bit nonce, and 32-bit block counter. Both XOR plaintext with a deterministic pseudorandom keystream: never reuse a nonce under the same key, and never let the block counter wrap. Original specification; RFC 8439 §§2.3, 4 and errata.

🔑 Key Exchange — Creating a Shared Secret
Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange → exhibit

Derives a shared secret over a public channel. Computing the shared group element is the computational Diffie–Hellman problem. Discrete logarithms provide one route to solving it; protocol security also needs authentication. Not a cipher — it does not encrypt data.
🔓 Public-Key Cryptography — Asymmetric Encryption & Signatures
RSA (Asymmetric) → exhibit

Rivest, Shamir, Adleman (1977). Two keys — public (encrypt) and private (decrypt) — based on arithmetic modulo a product of large primes; factoring that composite reveals the private key. Used in TLS, email, and digital signatures.
#️⃣ Hash Functions — One-Way Fingerprints
SHA-256 (Hash Function) → exhibit

Produces a fixed 256-bit digest of a message shorter than 2⁶⁴ bits. Preimage resistance means finding an input for a chosen digest should be computationally difficult; low-entropy inputs can still be guessed. Generic collision search takes roughly 2¹²⁸ work, rather than having a proven exact lower bound. Used in digital signatures, certificate verification, blockchain. A hash is not a reversible encryption scheme; guessed inputs can still be tested.
🔮 The Future — Post-Quantum Cryptography
Post-Quantum Cryptography

Sufficiently large, fault-tolerant quantum computers could break conventional RSA and Diffie–Hellman using Shor’s algorithm. NIST standardized the first three algorithms from its post-quantum project in 2024: ML-KEM (formerly CRYSTALS-Kyber, key encapsulation) and ML-DSA (formerly CRYSTALS-Dilithium, signatures), both based on structured lattice problems. NIST also standardized the hash-based SLH-DSA signature scheme in 2024. These are intended to resist known quantum attacks; they are not proofs against every future attack. The mathematics is on exhibit in the Hall of Foundations: lattices & the SVP, the closest-vector problem, Learning With Errors — an introduction to the mathematical setting of lattice-based standards — and polynomial rings.

Elliptic-Curve Signatures → exhibit

ECDSA signatures verify under a public key rather than concealing content; attribution to a person requires a trusted key association: a private scalar on an elliptic curve, a public point, and a signature anyone can check. Suitable 256-bit elliptic-curve groups and 3072-bit RSA are commonly compared at roughly 128 bits of classical security, under their respective assumptions. Security requires an appropriate curve and implementation. Reused or biased nonces, side-channel leakage, faults and validation errors can compromise a signer. Shor’s algorithm also applies to the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem. The group law is in the Hall of Foundations.

Threshold Secret Sharing → exhibit

Shamir’s Secret Sharing (1979) represents the secret in a finite field and samples the remaining polynomial coefficients independently and uniformly. With distinct nonzero share coordinates, k valid shares reconstruct the secret; fewer than k preserve its prior distribution. This information-theoretic property also holds against quantum computation, but does not protect stolen shares or faulty implementations. Threshold signing protocols can build on secret-sharing ideas but require additional protections; plain Shamir sharing does not authenticate shares or perform signatures.

Zero-Knowledge Proofs → exhibit

Zero-knowledge proofs combine a soundness guarantee with a specified bound on what the verifier can learn beyond the statement. The guarantee depends on the protocol and security model; toy protocols can have a visible nonzero soundness error. The succinct variants — zk-SNARKs and STARKs — validate private transactions and compress whole blocks of computation into a proof small enough to check on a blockchain. The Fiat–Shamir transformation connects certain interactive identification protocols to noninteractive proofs or signatures under additional assumptions. A signature is not automatically a general-purpose zero-knowledge proof.

🎓

The museum's final lesson: Many historical systems failed through structural weaknesses, key reuse, or operational mistakes; others are obsolete or unresolved. A correctly used one-time pad retains perfect secrecy. Modern cryptography adds computational security and public-key methods, while still depending on sound key management, and extends into protocols, signatures, and proofs.
