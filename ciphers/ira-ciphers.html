Why This Matters

This exhibit is about a body of real ciphertext and the man who read it. In 2001 the historian Tom Mahon found roughly three hundred documents containing encrypted messages in a Dublin archive — the papers of Moss Twomey, IRA chief of staff from 1926 to 1936. Between them they held about 1,300 individual cryptograms, mostly dispatches between headquarters and organisers in Britain and the United States, dating from the 1920s. Mahon brought in the American cryptanalyst Jim Gillogly, and together they published Decoding the IRA in 2008. Their book left one challenge unresolved; Richard Bean subsequently solved it in 2019.

Historical Context

The collection documents IRA correspondence during the 1920s. Dunin and Schmeh describe both columnar transpositions and a smaller set of Vigenère-encrypted passages embedded in otherwise readable letters, including an example dated 4 May 1923. The collection should not be described as using only one cipher.

For the Vigenère passages, Gillogly combined short samples to search for a reused key. The recovered keyword, GVZKLG, is TEAPOT transformed by a reversed alphabet. Ampersands represented Z in the ciphertext. This case shows why reused keys and operator conventions matter alongside the underlying cipher.

How the Cipher Worked

Most of the cryptograms used columnar transposition, the mechanism demonstrated here. The message is written into a grid of fixed line length; the columns are then read out in an order set by a keyword. No letter is ever replaced — every letter of the plaintext survives into the ciphertext, only its position changes. Single-letter frequencies cannot recover the column order, but they can help suggest a transposition and a likely language. Letter-group statistics help score candidate orders.

Operators also padded. In the message Gillogly works through as an example, the encipherer had inserted two columns of meaningless vowels to disguise the shape of the grid.

The demo above uses the museum's columnar engine. It is the right mechanism, but it is a clean-room model: it does not reproduce the padding conventions, the five-letter grouping, or the handwriting of the originals.

How It Was Broken
Hill climbing over column orders
Method: scored search over candidate column orders

Gillogly wrote hill-climbing software aimed at columnar transposition. In the worked example described by Dunin and Schmeh, he tried line lengths in the range of about eight to fifteen characters, ran the search around a hundred times from different random starting keys, and watched for the point at which recognisable English words began to fall out of a candidate solution. The approach is the same family this museum covers under hill climbing, but its success depends on message length, language scoring, transcription and the particular cipher conventions.

The final published challenge — solved in 2019
Richard Bean; damaged or incomplete ciphertext

This surviving 51-letter cryptogram was the unresolved challenge in Decoding the IRA (2008):

GTHOO RCSNM EOTDE TAEDI NRAHE
EBFNS INSGD AILLA YTTSE AOITDE

Bean’s reconstruction concerns a report about a raid and gelignite in Scotland. Codebreaking: A Practical Guide, by Elonka Dunin and Klaus Schmeh (2020), explains a missing L within the ciphertext and an omitted four-letter column, ETDS. A reconstructed 56-letter message, retaining the source’s THLS spelling, can be transposed with the equivalent key BCAFIEHGKDLJ; removing those five letters reproduces the displayed 51 letters. This is a reproducible reconstruction, not recovery of the original keyword.

Evidence and Reading
