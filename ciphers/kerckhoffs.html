Kerckhoffs's Principle

The most important rule in cryptography: assume your adversary knows everything about your system except the key.

Why This Matters

In La Cryptographie Militaire (1883), Auguste Kerckhoffs argued that a military cryptosystem should not require its design to remain secret. This separates a replaceable secret key from the mechanism. Published designs such as AES can receive independent scrutiny, but publication and key secrecy alone do not guarantee security.

The Six Requirements

Kerckhoffs stated six design criteria for military ciphers: (1) the system must be practically, if not mathematically, indecipherable; (2) the system must not require secrecy and can be stolen by the enemy without causing trouble; (3) the key must be easy to communicate and remember without written notes, and changeable at will; (4) the cipher must be applicable to telegraph correspondence; (5) the apparatus must be portable and operable by a single person; (6) the system must be easy to use. Criterion 2 is what posterity calls "Kerckhoffs's Principle."

Why Secrecy Through Obscurity Fails

Concealing a design can be an additional obstacle, but a robust cryptosystem should not depend on keeping that design secret. Keys can then be replaced without redesigning the whole system. This principle does not require a separate key for every message, and key secrecy alone does not guarantee a flawed algorithm is secure.

Enigma and Knowledge of the Mechanism

Enigma’s design and wiring were important to its analysis, but learning the machine did not automatically reveal every daily key. Captured key material, operator practices, cribs and structural weaknesses all mattered. The lesson is to evaluate security with the design known, rather than equating machine capture with instant decryption.

The Modern Implication

NIST began the AES development process in 1997, selected Rijndael in October 2000 and published FIPS 197 in November 2001. The public competition invited analysis of candidate algorithms. AES illustrates evaluation with the algorithm known, alongside the continuing need for appropriate modes, key management and sound implementations.
