Interactive Exhibit

Type any letter A–Z and the widget will show its pigpen symbol description (which grid, which cell, with-dot or without). Try short messages too. Track B — illustrative; for hands-on encoding see the Pigpen Cipher exhibit.

Why This Matters

Pigpen represents letters with parts of grids and X-shaped figures, often using dots to distinguish a second set. It appears in fraternal inscriptions and in recreational puzzles. A symbol alphabet can convey affiliation as well as obscure a message; those purposes should be assessed for each object.

Historical Context

Kahn discusses James Leeson’s 1794 gravestone in New York as a documented Masonic example. Claims of a single London standardisation in the 1730s, a medieval Templar origin or specific Hellfire Club use are not established by the evidence cited here. The widget shows a common modern layout, not a transcription of that gravestone.

Technical Notes

With a secret alphabet, pigpen behaves as monoalphabetic substitution and retains letter and word patterns. With a known fixed alphabet, it is immediately reversible by lookup. Recognising the style does not always identify the exact assignment, and short inscriptions need not yield to frequency analysis alone.

Modern Echo

Pigpen-style alphabets are useful recreational exercises because their encoding rule can be drawn and learned quickly. The museum’s Pigpen Cipher exhibit uses text stand-ins and a separately labelled drawn variant. Neither widget establishes that a specific film, lodge or historical individual used that exact alphabet.
