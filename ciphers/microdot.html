Why This Matters

Microdots hide a greatly reduced photographic image in an ordinary object or document. Intelligence services used them during the twentieth century, but microphotography predates World War II. Concealing a message and encrypting its contents are separate operations.

Historical Context

During WWII, the Abwehr (German military intelligence) operated a network of agents in the United States who used microdots to pass intelligence about US military production back to Hamburg. The network was broken in 1941 when double-agent William Sebold led the FBI to the New York cell. Federal agents discovered microdots concealed in a magazine — tiny specks that, under a microscope, revealed typewritten correspondence. J. Edgar Hoover publicly recounted the discovery in 1946, once exposure no longer helped the enemy.

How It Worked

A document can be photographed onto a tiny piece of high-resolution film and concealed in a carrier. Reading it requires suitable magnification. Sizes and equipment vary; this browser does not model optical resolution, manufacture a physical microdot or provide encryption.

Detection and Countermeasures
Photographic enlargement + mail examination
Complexity: Moderate — requires systematic examination of all correspondence

An examiner who suspects a microdot can inspect a carrier using magnification and other forensic techniques. Finding the carrier may defeat concealment, but any independent encryption of its contents must still be addressed separately.

Modern Descendants

Microdots and digital steganography share the goal of concealing the presence of information. Their physical and digital mechanisms differ; twentieth-century microdot development was not the invention of steganography itself.
