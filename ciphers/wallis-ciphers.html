Why This Matters

John Wallis (1616–1703) combined mathematics with cryptanalytic work for successive English governments. David Kahn’s account places his first successful reading in early 1643: a relatively simple substitution that Wallis said he solved in a few hours. A much harder numerical cipher took him about three months. These contrasting cases do not support a universal two-hour recovery time. Wallis became Savilian Professor of Geometry at Oxford in 1649 and continued cryptanalytic work after the Restoration and under William and Mary.

How It Works

Wallis worked on substitution ciphers and nomenclators combining letter values with codes for words or names. Their formats varied. The demo uses a museum-invented 64-word codebook, homophonic letters and explicit mode markers; it is not a transcription of a particular Royalist key. Frequency, repeated patterns and context can guide recovery, but a successful attack depends on the material available.

How It Was Broken
Wallis’s frequency-and-context attack
Difficulty varies with the cipher, traffic and available context

Repeated contexts, likely names and partially recovered letter values can help reconstruct codebook entries. A group’s low frequency does not by itself prove it is a person or place, and the existence of Wallis’s work does not establish that Royalists made no counter-cryptanalytic effort.

Operational attack opportunities
Scope: General lessons, not a claim about every dispatch

A captured key, predictable wording or plaintext mixed with ciphertext can help an analyst. Those are general attack opportunities; they are not evidence that every Royalist message shared the same weakness. Specific claims about captured keys or correspondence need a dated document.

Wallis’s longevity
Experience: Work across successive governments

Wallis’s later correspondence records demanding work on newly encountered ciphers, including weeks spent on difficult cases. His experience helped, but did not guarantee immediate success. He trained his grandson William Blencowe, who assumed cryptanalytic duties after Wallis’s death in 1703.

Research Trail

Wallis sits inside the same nomenclator tradition mapped in Cryptiana, especially the archival work on 16th- and 17th-century diplomatic cipher practice. Read him in sequence with Argenti for the design side and with Arnold-André for the later book-cipher branch of state secrecy.

Evidence and Reading

David Kahn, The Codebreakers: The Comprehensive History of Secret Communication from Ancient Times to the Internet, revised edition (1996), chapter 5, Wallis and the English deciphering service. Kahn contrasts Wallis’s easy first substitution with later work lasting days, weeks or months.
