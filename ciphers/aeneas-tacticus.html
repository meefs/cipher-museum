Aeneas Tacticus Water-Clock Signal Code

The Greek general who wrote the earliest surviving treatise on military communications.

AuthorAeneas Tacticus (Aeneas the Tactician)
Year~350 BCE
WorkLost work quoted by Polybius, Histories 10.44
MethodSynchronized water clocks selecting preset messages
Modern LessonSynchronization enables signaling; it does not itself provide secrecy

Why This Matters

Aeneas wrote about military communication in the fourth century BCE. His water-clock scheme survives in Polybius, Histories 10.44–45: matched vessels selected prearranged military messages. It was a signaling code, not an alphabetic encryption system.

Historical Context

The surviving Poliorketika discusses concealed messages. The water-clock account comes from a different, lost work, quoted by Polybius. Keeping those sources distinct avoids attributing every later Greek signaling method to Aeneas.

How the Water Clock Worked

Sender and receiver prepared matching earthenware vessels with floating corks and marked rods. Torch signals coordinated the start and stop of draining. The rod level indicated a preset message. Polybius emphasized the limitation: an unexpected event could not be expressed unless it was already on the list.

Security Properties
Visibility, not secrecy
Complexity: None

An observer who knew the message list and timing could interpret the signals. Synchronization alone supplied no encryption. Polybius next describes a separate alphabetic torch system; that should not be merged into the water-clock mechanism.
