Why This Matters

Arnold negotiated with the British through John André, offering to deliver West Point. Their secret correspondence is an important example of the relationship between book codes and operational concealment. A cover name or cipher convention should be tied to a particular surviving letter rather than invented to make the story vivid.

The correspondence used several book codes. Kahn describes an initial scheme based on volume I of the fifth Oxford edition of William Blackstone’s Commentaries on the Laws of England, followed by Nathan Bailey’s dictionary and then a smaller, unidentified dictionary. The codes changed; they should not be presented as one fixed reference book throughout the affair.

André was captured near Tarrytown on September 23, 1780, carrying documents concerning West Point. He was executed on October 2; Arnold escaped. The New York State Archives’ documentary exhibition supports the capture account. It does not establish the additional claim that the cipher’s reference book or key was found in his boot.

How It Works

Both correspondents own identical copies of an agreed reference book. To encrypt a word, the sender finds it in the book and writes down a triple page.line.word. To decrypt, the receiver looks up that triple in their own copy and recovers the plaintext.

The reference book may be public, but its identity, edition, and indexing convention can be secrets shared by the correspondents. A published book is still key material in that role, and both search and capture may compromise it. Merely searching a courier does not automatically reveal every missing parameter.

In the Blackstone scheme, words absent from the book could be spelled using letter positions, marked by a stroke through the final number. Later dictionary arrangements used different conventions. The particular book, edition and procedure must therefore be identified before a numeric group can be interpreted.

The demo above uses a deterministic 240-word “book” organised as 12 pages × 5 lines × 4 words. Words not in the book fall through to per-letter triples on “page 14”, bracketed by sentinel triples 13.1.1 and 15.1.1 — invented software conventions, not historical Arnold–André procedures.

How It Was Compromised
Capture of the courier (23 September 1780)
Complexity: Three militiamen, an unscheduled patrol

André was returning from West Point in civilian clothes; the circumstances of his mission and capture led to his trial as a spy. John Paulding, David Williams, and Isaac Van Wart stopped him near Tarrytown, found the documents in his boot, and turned him over to the Continental Army. He was tried, convicted, and hanged within ten days.

Document capture exposed the plot
Evidence: captured West Point documents; no key capture established here

The documented capture exposed incriminating documents. That is different from proving that all earlier cipher correspondence was deciphered using a captured key. Book-code security depends on the chosen text, conventions, traffic, and attacker knowledge; it cannot be certified from this operational episode.

Satoshi Tomokiyo's Cryptiana notes on the Arnold-André correspondence are among the clearest specialist introductions to how Revolutionary-era book ciphers were actually organised on the page. They pair well with this exhibit because they show the operational side of book codes rather than only the treason narrative.
