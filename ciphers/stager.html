Stager Cipher

Historical Only

Union telegraph routes, codewords and changing wartime keys

Historical Union route-code system; wartime operational success is not a mathematical security guarantee.

InventorAnson Stager · US Military Telegraph Corps
Year1861
Key TypeRoute transposition + code words
Broken ByWartime success reported; not a mathematical guarantee
Modern LessonRoute transposition as message obfuscation

Why This Matters

Stager’s route-code systems protected important Union military telegraph traffic. They combined whole-word transposition with codeword substitutions and nulls. Accounts of their operational success do not establish that every sensitive Union message used them or that no intercepted message was ever understood.

Historical Context

Stager developed a military route system in 1861 after earlier work for Ohio’s governor. McClellan’s use helped spread it. Keeping ciphertext in ordinary words reduced telegraphic copying errors; codewords could conceal important names and terms even after a route was recovered.

The procedures changed during the war. Kahn describes a series of twelve systems and reports that Cipher No. 4 used twelve pages of routes and thirty-six pages listing 1,608 codewords. One dated example in his account is Lincoln’s 1 June 1863 dispatch under Cipher No. 9. The diagram below is a separate teaching illustration.

How It Works
Illustrative entries, not an authenticated key:
Step 1: Code word substitution
        RAPTURE = Louisville, KY
        CAMDEN = Maj-Gen Thomas
        ALVORD = General Buell

Step 2: Write message in N columns
        (N determined by commencement word)

Step 3: Read columns by prearranged route
        'up 1, down 6, up 2, down 3, up 5, down 4'

Step 4: Insert null words at column ends
        to disguise column boundaries

Illustrative route grid, not a transcription of a dated dispatch: The route and column count were specified by the first 'commencement word' of the message.

The historical description and the word-fragment diagram therefore describe different levels of the subject.

How It Was Broken
✅

Operational history is not a proof of security. Kahn describes the Union systems’ wartime success. An absence of a documented Confederate solution does not establish impossibility. A captured vocabulary or route could expose affected messages without an abstract cryptanalytic breakthrough.

Post-War Cryptanalysis
Historical reconstruction from messages and records

Published operator memoirs, surviving codebooks and telegrams support later reconstruction. Evidence must be tied to a particular key and dispatch. The letter-route widget shows reversible transposition; successfully solving it says little about the effort required for a historical word-code message.
