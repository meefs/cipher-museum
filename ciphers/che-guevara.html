Che Guevara’s Checkerboard and Pad

Historical Only

A checkerboard converts letters into digits; a secret digit pad supplies a separate encryption layer. This is not the full VIC construction.

Historical checkerboard-plus-pad system; the widget compares a condensed VIC model, not Guevara’s historical pad system.

Why This Matters

Accounts of Guevara’s communications describe a straddling checkerboard with a digit one-time pad. The CrypTool researcher’s reconstruction illustrates that combination. Sharing a checkerboard component with VIC does not make the two complete systems equivalent.

Historical Context

VIC derives key material and applies transpositions. A one-time-pad system instead requires independent, uniformly random secret digits, never reused, as long as the digit message. These are materially different security assumptions. The museum has not established the former claim that Guevara received a particular KGB cipher course in Moscow.

How the Straddling Checkerboard Works

A straddling checkerboard gives some letters one-digit codes and others two-digit codes. This is a reversible encoding, not a guarantee against frequency analysis. Adding fresh secret pad digits modulo 10 can provide one-time-pad secrecy if the pad conditions are actually met.

About this demo: The widget uses the museum’s condensed VIC engine, including chain addition and two transpositions. It is a comparison exercise, not a reconstruction of Guevara’s pad system. Its keyword-generated digits are not a one-time pad.

How It Was Exposed
Physical capture
Complexity: Trivial with materials in hand

Captured documents can compromise a communications system without breaking its mathematics. A U.S. memorandum dated July 15, 1967 already discusses captured guerrilla documents; exposure cannot all be assigned to Guevara’s October capture. This exhibit makes no claim of ciphertext-only key recovery or of a verified 24-hour transfer of cipher materials to Langley.
