Agnes Meyer Driscoll

1889 – 1971

Agnes Meyer Driscoll joined the Naval Reserve in 1918 and later worked as a civilian cryptanalyst. Her career included a short period at the Hebern company, so it was not uninterrupted Navy service. Kevin Wade Johnson’s The Neglected Giant: Agnes Meyer Driscoll (2015), pp. 3–4, 33–37, documents her education, retirement and death. She contributed to attacks on Japanese naval codebooks and the M-1 machine. In 1940 she made early progress on JN-25, then moved to work on German naval Enigma; the unsuccessful wartime effort was not a three-year JN-25 assignment. She retired in 1959.

Why This Person Matters

Driscoll was an important early naval cryptanalyst and mentor, including to Joseph Rochefort. Her work belongs to a collective institutional history, not a claim that every Pacific codebreaking success was hers. The NSA biography distinguishes her Japanese-code achievements from her later German naval Enigma work.

Red Book and Blue Book

Driscoll worked on the Japanese Navy’s Red Book and Blue Book systems in the interwar period. Codebook recovery, traffic analysis, and attacks on superencipherment were different parts of this work. Exact entry counts and a single uniform attack procedure should not be inferred from the color names alone; the declassified naval history linked below describes the successive systems.

Breaking the M-1 Machine

The NSA credits Driscoll with leading the 1935 attack on the Japanese M-1, called ORANGE by American analysts. This demonstrated her ability to attack machine-generated traffic. This was a separate system from PURPLE; success against one machine does not establish the mechanism or attack used against another.

Teacher of Codebreakers

Driscoll trained and collaborated with Navy analysts whose later work supported American signals intelligence. Teaching mattered alongside individual solutions: analysts needed methods for organizing intercepted traffic, testing hypotheses, and recovering code values. This contribution does not require claiming that no cryptanalytic curriculum existed elsewhere.
