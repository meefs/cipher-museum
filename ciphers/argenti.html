Why This Matters

Giovanni Battista Argenti and Matteo Argenti served the papal cipher office in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. Surviving keys and manuscripts document their work on diplomatic ciphers. Matteo succeeded his uncle Giovanni Battista in 1591; Marcello was Matteo’s younger brother, not his nephew.

Matteo’s manuscripts discuss homophones, nulls, codewords, and cryptanalysis. Aloys Meister published papal cryptographic material in 1906. Satoshi Tomokiyo’s source-based analysis distinguishes Matteo’s service in 1591–1605 from his later compilation of material. These techniques were not all invented at once in a 1591 handbook.

Nomenclators remained important in European diplomacy for centuries, with substantial variation among keys and administrations. The Argenti documents are valuable examples, not evidence that every later chancery or the earlier Babington correspondence copied a single Argenti pattern.

How It Works

An Argenti cipher has three layers stacked on top of one another:

  1. Homophonic alphabet. Each plaintext letter is assigned several cipher symbols (typically 2–4 for common letters, 1–2 for rare). The choice among those symbols depends on the key and procedure; rotation in the widget below is a teaching convention.
  2. Nomenclator. A short list of named persons, places, offices, and frequent words gets dedicated codes that bypass the alphabet entirely — “the Pope”, “the Emperor”, “Paris”, etc.
  3. Nulls. Meaningless symbols sprinkled at agreed positions to confuse a cryptanalyst counting frequencies.

The demo alternates between two numeric homophones per letter. This is a deliberately simple model of multiple representations, not an exact Argenti key or selection procedure. Giving every letter equally many homophones does not flatten its combined language frequency. Try AAAAAA with the demo seed ARGENTI: it produces 66 40 66 40 66 40. These numbers belong to this invented teaching key.

How It Was Broken
Single-letter frequency attack (limited)
Complexity: Very high against a properly-designed nomenclator

When each letter has two homophones, their combined counts retain the letter’s frequency. Analysts can test groupings using context and probable words. Length influences the available evidence, but there is no blanket rule that every long note falls and every short diplomatic note is safe.

Compromise of the codebook itself
Complexity: Espionage problem, not cryptanalytic

A stolen keylist can expose messages independently of cryptanalysis. The sources identified here do not establish that capture was the most frequent cause of compromise for every nomenclator or that the Vatican followed a uniform annual replacement cycle.

Eventually obsolete
Later cryptanalysis and changing practice

Wallis and other professional analysts attacked diplomatic nomenclators, but their use did not end around 1700. Codebooks and related mixed systems continued alongside other methods well beyond that date.

Research Trail

For the Vatican cryptographic tradition, David Kahn's chapter on papal ciphers remains the standard narrative entry point, but the best specialist trail runs through Satoshi Tomokiyo's Cryptiana essays and archival notes on early-modern nomenclators. Use this exhibit alongside Wallis Ciphers and Mary Stuart's Castelnau letters to follow the same diplomatic cipher family across three centuries.
