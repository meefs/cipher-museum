SIGABA (ECM Mark II)

Historical Only

An American WWII machine with fifteen rotors and irregular stepping; no successful Axis cryptanalytic break is documented in the cited public record.

Never publicly broken by cryptanalysis in wartime service; obsolete today.

OriginWilliam F. Friedman & Frank Rowlett (US Army SIS); Laurance Safford (US Navy OP-20-G)
YearApproved 1940; in service 1941–1959
Key Type15 rotors arranged as 3 banks of 5 (cipher × 5, control × 5, index × 5)
Broken ByNo documented successful enemy wartime cryptanalytic break
Modern LessonRotor-control design changes the structure available to an analyst

Why This Matters

SIGABA used separate control and index rotor banks to decide which cipher rotors advanced. Individual stepping patterns can repeat; the design does not promise that no pattern occurs twice. Its operational record differs from a mathematical proof of security, and standard Enigma stepping itself includes a double-step rather than being a perfect odometer.

In the public record, this system was never publicly broken by cryptanalysis in wartime service. That historical record does not establish security today; this obsolete system is presented for historical study.

Historical Context

SIGABA was used for important American military communications during World War II. Its five cipher rotors were controlled by two additional banks of five rotors. No successful Axis cryptanalytic reading is established in the public record cited here. Numbers describing possible settings are not automatically equivalent to bits of attack resistance.

How It Works

Three banks of five rotors. The cipher bank performs the actual substitution. The control bank steps regularly, odometer-fashion. The control bank's outputs feed the index bank — five rotors whose contacts encode the question ‘which of the five cipher rotors steps next?’ The result: any given keystroke advances 1–4 of the cipher rotors in a pattern that depends on the entire current rotor state. The simulation in this exhibit models that pseudorandom advance and the resulting per-keystroke substitution.

Why It Held
No measured attack-cost estimate is established by this exhibit

SIGABA differs from Enigma in both stepping and signal path, including the absence of an Enigma-style reflector. Those differences matter to attacks. They do not justify saying that analysis has “nothing to grip,” or that absence of a wartime break proves a machine secure for later use.
