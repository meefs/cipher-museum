Why This Matters

Plaintext autokey extends a primer with the message itself. It avoids cycling a short keyword at a fixed period, but letters and passages can still repeat. This changes the attack surface rather than proving 350 years of field security. Joshua Holden’s The Mathematics of Secrets: Cryptography from Caesar Ciphers to Digital Encryption (2017), §5.3, distinguishes several historical self-keying constructions.

Historical Context

Cardano, Bellaso, and Vigenère discussed different self-keying and polyalphabetic ideas. The familiar repeating-key “Vigenère” cipher is associated with Bellaso’s 1553 work; Vigenère’s 1586 book included autokey constructions. The widget teaches the specified keyword-plus-plaintext model, rather than reproducing every feature of those historical systems.

How It Works

Pick a short keyword K. Encrypt the first |K| plaintext letters with K under a Vigenère shift. Then take the plaintext itself, prepend K, and use that string as the running key for the rest of the message. Decryption recovers K-many letters first, then uses those recovered plaintext letters as the key for the next stretch — and so on. There is no fixed cycled keyword after the primer. The appended message still contains language structure and may repeat.

How It Was Broken
Crib Propagation
Crib propagation is straightforward; finding a correct crib or primer can require substantial search

The extended key is related to the plaintext and is not independent random material. A guessed plaintext letter reveals a later key letter, allowing the guess to propagate at a distance equal to the primer length. Crib testing and language scoring can exploit this structure; there is no universal guarantee of recovery within a specified number of trials.
