Why This Matters

Herodotus’s Histories 5.35 describes Histiaeus, detained at Susa, urging Aristagoras to revolt. With the roads guarded, he had an enslaved messenger’s head shaved and marked, waited for the hair to grow, then sent him to Miletus with instructions to have his head shaved again. This is an ancient literary account of concealment; the visualization is not independent evidence for every detail of the journey.

Historical Context

The episode appears in Herodotus, Histories 5.35. Aeneas Tacticus wrote in the following century; his discussions of concealed messages belong to a separate source, not to Herodotus’s account. The surviving narrative does not establish a universal “first use” of steganography.

Cryptography vs Steganography

Encryption aims to hide a message’s meaning from someone who sees its ciphertext; steganography aims to hide the presence of a message. Herodotus describes concealment without specifying an encryption algorithm. Finding the concealed marks and interpreting them are separate tasks. Modern systems can encrypt a payload before embedding it in cover material.

The Modern Heir: Steganography Today

Digital steganography can embed information in images or audio, while covert channels can exploit other observable behaviour. Security depends on what an observer can detect, inspect and modify. An overlooked channel is not automatically secure, and finding a hidden payload need not reveal its meaning if it is separately encrypted.
