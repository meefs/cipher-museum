Fialka M-125 Soviet 10-rotor machine · 1956

Historical Only

Ten rotors, irregular stepping, and a punched-card substitution: a distinct Cold War machine with important differences from Enigma.

Never publicly broken by cryptanalysis in operational service; obsolete today.

The interactive is a Fialka-inspired, seeded 26-letter rotor model. It does not reproduce the M-125's 30-contact alphabet, authentic rotor wiring, key-card configuration, or stepping system.

Why This Matters

Fialka M-125 entered service in 1956 and was used by the Soviet Union and allied countries. Variants had different keyboards and country-specific rotor wiring. It was one of several communications-security systems, not the device through which every Warsaw Pact signal passed.

The machine changed several features familiar from Enigma, including the wheel count, stepping, and input substitution. Improvements to particular weaknesses do not prove that a design has no others.

In the public record, this system was never publicly broken by cryptanalysis in operational service. That historical record does not establish security today; this obsolete system is presented for historical study.

How It Works

Adjacent rotors move in opposite directions. Mechanical pin patterns control irregular stepping; not every wheel necessarily advances on each keypress. The punched card supplies a substitution rather than setting the rotor order, ring positions, or stepping pins. See the Crypto Museum’s documented machines and technical reference.

Three further design choices matter:

  • Self-encipherment is possible. Fialka’s electrical arrangement permits a letter to encipher to itself, unlike military Enigma.
  • Punched-card substitution. A daily card replaces the plugboard’s role; the other machine settings remain separate.
  • Thirty-contact alphabet. The later M-125-3 supports mixed letters, numbers, and punctuation through operating modes; 30 contacts do not individually represent all those symbols at once.
