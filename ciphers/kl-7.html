KL-7 ADONIS NATO eight-rotor machine · 1952

Historical Only

Eight rotors, irregular stepping, re-entrant alphabet — the rotor machine NATO trusted with everything until John Walker handed the keys to the Soviets.

Never publicly broken by cryptanalysis in operational service; stolen keys exposed traffic.

The interactive is a KL-7-inspired, seeded 26-letter rotor model. It does not reproduce ADONIS rotor wiring, notch rings, stepping, or re-entry circuitry.

Why This Matters

The KL-7, code-named ADONIS, was the rotor machine that carried NATO’s most sensitive traffic for almost two decades. It encrypted everything from US Navy submarine orders to NATO command messages. By 1965 the United States alone had over 25,000 KL-7 units in service.

No public cryptanalytic break of operational KL-7 traffic is documented. Operationally, it suffered the most damaging spy compromise in modern American history: Navy Chief Warrant Officer John Walker sold its key lists to the Soviets from 1967 to 1985, allowing the KGB to read US Navy traffic in near real time.

In the public record, this system was never publicly broken by cryptanalysis in operational service. Keys stolen by the Walker spy ring nevertheless exposed operational traffic. That historical record does not establish security today; this obsolete system is presented for historical study.

How It Works

Eight rotors form the cipher stack. The fourth is stationary; seven step under the control of notch rings. Each has 36 contacts: 26 carry the alphabet, while ten feed output back through the stack. This re-entry can send a signal through the rotors several times before it reaches a letter output.

There is no Enigma-style reflector. Encryption and decryption reverse the signal direction; the design does not impose Enigma’s no-self-encryption restriction. These features describe the historical machine, not a proof of security or a specification of this simplified widget.

Sources: Crypto Museum’s examined machine and re-entry explanation; the declassified KAM-1 maintenance manual identifies the stationary fourth rotor.

How It Was (Not) Broken
No public cryptanalytic break
Complexity: Believed infeasible without key material

No public wartime-style cryptanalytic defeat is established by the evidence cited here. Absence of a published break is not a proof that the design is secure, and it does not erase compromises through stolen key material.

The Walker spy ring (1967–1985)
Complexity: N/A — keys were stolen, not derived

John Walker, a US Navy communications watch officer, photographed KL-7 key lists and sold them to the KGB through a network that included his brother, son, and a friend. The Soviets read US Navy traffic for the entire period — the most damaging American cipher compromise of the Cold War. Walker was arrested in 1985.
