MIT Mystery Hunt

An annual puzzle hunt at MIT (since 1981) where teams solve dozens of layered cryptographic, linguistic, and lateral-thinking puzzles to find a hidden coin somewhere on campus.

Why This Matters

MIT’s Mystery Hunt combines puzzles, team collaboration and a search for a hidden coin. It began in 1981. Team sizes, puzzle counts and finishing times vary by year; the event is not restricted to cryptography, and its puzzle difficulty is not a measure of cryptographic security.

Historical Context

MIT News attributes the first 1981 hunt to graduate student Brad Schaefer. The winning team earns the opportunity to design the next hunt. Its report on 2015 describes more than 70 registered teams and 2,200 participants, with 158 online puzzles plus events and runarounds. Those are figures for that year, not fixed features of every hunt.

Technical Notes

A meta-puzzle uses answers or other information from earlier puzzles. Cipher recognition may be one step, alongside wordplay, mathematics, images or physical tasks. For example, a designer could use earlier answers to supply a substitution key; this is an illustrative design idea, not an attributed historical Hunt puzzle. The small widget above is an original museum exercise.

Modern Echo

Puzzle hunts encourage collaboration, hypothesis testing and clear records of intermediate results. Those habits can help with historical cipher research. Solving a designed puzzle, however, does not establish that an encryption scheme used in a real protocol is secure or broken.
