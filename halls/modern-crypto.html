Hall XI · 1976 – Present

Modern Cryptography

Different cryptographic tasks, constructions and security assumptions

The earlier halls distinguish cryptanalytic breaks, operational misuse, obsolescence, and unresolved systems; a correctly used one-time pad remains unbreakable. The constructions here serve different purposes. DES and AES are symmetric block ciphers; DES is now unsafe because its 56-bit key is too small. RSA supports distinct public-key encryption and signature schemes, with appropriate encoding and padding such as OAEP and PSS. Diffie–Hellman is key agreement, and SHA-256 is a hash function. These building blocks explain encryption, shared-secret establishment, signatures, and integrity; none alone provides a complete secure system.

For the broader landscape — ChaCha20, ECDH, post-quantum standardisation, and the cipher-vs-protocol taxonomy — see the Modern Cryptography wing. For the design rule that underpins every card in this hall, see Kerckhoffs's Principle.

Exhibit 59 1977 · NBS / NSA
DES (Data Encryption Standard)

DES was standardized in 1977. Its 56-bit key became vulnerable to practical exhaustive search, demonstrated by EFF’s Deep Crack in 1998.

Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange

Diffie–Hellman derives a shared group element over a public channel. Authentication, appropriate groups and key derivation are needed for secure protocol use.

Exhibit 61 1977 · MIT
RSA

RSA uses a public modulus and exponents to support public-key constructions. Secure encryption and signatures need specified padding, adequate parameters and correct implementation.

Exhibit 62 2001 · FIPS 197
AES (Advanced Encryption Standard)

AES standardises a 128-bit block cipher with 128-, 192- or 256-bit keys. Secure use also requires an appropriate mode, key management and implementation.

Secure (Modern) FIPS 197

Exhibit 63 2002 · NSA / NIST
SHA-256

SHA-256 produces a 256-bit digest for messages shorter than 2^64 bits. Hashing has distinct roles in signatures, integrity checks and other protocols; a bare hash is not authentication.

Exhibit 159 1979 · Adi Shamir
Shamir's Secret Sharing

Shamir’s finite-field construction lets k valid shares reconstruct a secret. Fewer shares preserve its prior distribution when coefficients are chosen uniformly and independently with distinct nonzero share coordinates.

The museum’s closing lesson: Modern cryptography specifies security goals, attack models and assumptions. Public designs, suitable parameters and careful implementations matter together. Historical weaknesses do not imply that every classical system is broken, or that modern primitives are secure under every use.
