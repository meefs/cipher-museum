The American Civil War Gallery

The telegraph transformed warfare — and cryptography with it

The American Civil War made extensive use of electric telegraphy for military command. It was not the first war to use telegraphs. Operators had to balance speed, interception risks and key distribution; the three systems here show different approaches rather than a single cause of the war’s outcome.

Telegraphy and interception: Electrical messages could cross long distances quickly, while intermediaries and wiretaps created security risks. Sensitive military traffic used codes and ciphers, but not every telegram was encrypted.

The Cryptographic Balance of Power

Contrasting Systems and Operational Evidence

Union Cryptography
  • Stager Cipher: Purpose-built for telegraph security. Route transposition + code-word substitution evolved continuously throughout the war, through a series of numbered cipher books ending with Cipher No. 4 (1865).
  • Professional management: The U.S. Military Telegraph Corps, under Stager and Eckert, controlled all cipher usage. Operators were trained and supervised.
  • Reported operational success: Historical accounts credit the Union system’s protection of traffic. This does not prove mathematical immunity or establish the fate of every intercepted telegram.
Confederate Cryptography
  • Repeating-key substitution: Confederate use preceded Kasiski’s 1863 publication. Documented Union solutions do not establish that the analysts used his method.
  • Key reuse: A recovered key could expose other traffic using that key. It did not automatically reveal every Confederate military message.
  • Dictionary codes: Correspondents needed matching editions and lookup conventions. Kahn documents specific dictionary arrangements; an unsupported story about two generals using mismatched editions is not evidence.
🏛️

Reading the evidence: Word-route ciphers, codeword substitutions and dictionary references are different procedures. The linked exhibits distinguish historical records from their synthetic browser examples. Source: David Kahn, The Codebreakers: The Comprehensive History of Secret Communication from Ancient Times to the Internet, revised edition (1996), Chapter 7, “Crises of the Union.”
