Hall I · World Origins · ~1900 BCE - ~1350 CE

World Origins of Cryptography

Where secrecy began across civilizations: Egypt, Hebrew, Greek, Roman, Indian, and Arabic traditions

Early writing traditions include concealment, signaling, language decipherment and deliberate encipherment. These are related topics, not interchangeable evidence of encryption. This hall identifies the historical sources and the limits of each modern demonstration.

Ancient Egypt Ancient Israel Ancient Greece Ancient Rome Classical India Abbasid Baghdad
📜

What this hall teaches: The surviving sources describe different problems: hiding a message, signalling it, substituting symbols and deciphering an unfamiliar script. Similarity between methods does not by itself establish a shared origin or direct transmission between cultures.

🧭

Suggested route: Start with Egyptian Substitution and the Rosetta Stone to separate unusual writing from language decipherment. Continue through Histiaeus, Scytale, Atbash and Caesar, then compare al-Kindi’s documented cryptanalytic methods.

What This Hall Teaches

Foundational Concepts

Modular Arithmetic

The Caesar cipher wraps around the alphabet using modulo 26. (position + shift) mod 26. Modular arithmetic appears throughout cryptography; AES uses binary finite-field operations rather than addition modulo 26.

Coordinate Encoding

Polybius’s coordinate notation separates a symbol into row and column information. Later fractionating ciphers rearrange such coordinates; modern bitslicing is a different implementation technique.

Frequency Analysis (the weakness)

Fixed substitutions preserve symbol counts while relabeling them. Frequency comparisons can suggest a key, especially with longer text, but short samples may not follow average language frequencies.
